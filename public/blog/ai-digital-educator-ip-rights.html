The Impact of the Expanding Use of Artificial Intelligence as a Digital Educator on the Intellectual Property Rights of Content Creators

Walid Mohamed Abu Al-Ela January 2026 9 min read 9th Scientific Conference on IP

As artificial intelligence systems increasingly serve as "digital educators" — trained on vast corpora of human-created content — a fundamental legal question emerges: whose rights are being taught, and at whose expense? This paper examines the tension between AI development and the intellectual property rights of the content creators whose works form the foundation of these systems.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of generative artificial intelligence has introduced a legal paradox. AI systems are, by their nature, students of human knowledge — they learn from what humans have written, drawn, composed, and created. In this sense, they function as "digital educators": systems that absorb, process, and reproduce patterns from an enormous corpus of copyrighted and protected works.

This dynamic creates an unprecedented legal question: when an AI system is trained on millions of copyrighted works, does that training constitute an infringement of the rights of the original creators? And if so, how should the law respond?

This paper — presented at the 9th Scientific Conference on Intellectual Property at the National Institute of Intellectual Property — argues that the current legal framework, both in Egypt and internationally, is not yet equipped to answer this question adequately. A new, balanced approach is needed — one that protects creators while allowing innovation to continue.

The "Digital Educator" Paradigm

The term "digital educator" is used here to describe AI systems that are trained on large datasets of human-created content for the purpose of learning patterns, styles, and information. This includes:

  • Large Language Models (LLMs) — trained on books, articles, websites, and other text.
  • Image generation models — trained on millions of photographs, illustrations, and artworks.
  • Music and audio models — trained on recorded music and compositions.
  • Code generation models — trained on open-source and proprietary code repositories.

What these systems share is a common dependency: they all rely on content created by human authors. Without that content, they cannot function. Yet in most cases, the original creators:

  • were not asked for permission;
  • received no compensation;
  • were not informed that their work was being used;
  • and have no practical way to opt out after the fact.

The core tension

AI systems learn from human creativity. Human creators, meanwhile, are increasingly competing with — and in some cases being replaced by — systems built on their own work. This is not simply an economic issue; it is a fundamental question about the purpose and scope of intellectual property law.

IP Challenges Posed by AI Training

3.1 The Reproduction Question

When an AI is trained on a copyrighted work, does that training constitute "reproduction" within the meaning of copyright law? Under most legal systems — including Egyptian Law No. 82 of 2002 on the Protection of Intellectual Property Rights — the exclusive right of reproduction belongs to the author.

The technical reality is that AI training involves creating temporary copies of works during processing. Whether these copies fall within existing exceptions (such as temporary reproduction exceptions) remains legally unsettled.

3.2 The Derivative Works Question

More complex still is the question of derivative works. When an AI system generates new output based on patterns learned from training data, is that output a "derivative work" of the original inputs? The answer is not straightforward — AI outputs are transformations, not copies, but they may still bear substantial similarity to protected works.

3.3 The Moral Rights Question

In civil law jurisdictions — including Egypt — authors enjoy moral rights (droit moral) that are distinct from economic rights. These include the right to attribution, the right to integrity of the work, and the right to object to derogatory treatment. AI training and output raise serious questions about whether moral rights are being respected.

3.4 The Opt-Out Problem

Even where creators wish to exclude their works from AI training, the practical mechanisms for doing so are inadequate. Most creators discover their work has been used only after the AI model has been released — by which point retraction is impossible.

The Egyptian Legal Context

Egyptian intellectual property law — primarily Law No. 82 of 2002 — was drafted in an era before generative AI existed. It contains no explicit provisions addressing AI training or AI-generated content.

However, several existing provisions of Egyptian law are relevant:

  • Article 148 — grants authors the exclusive right to reproduce their works.
  • Article 149 — grants authors the right to authorize or prohibit derivative works.
  • Article 143 — affirms the moral rights of authors.
  • Article 170 — provides exceptions and limitations, including for certain educational and non-commercial uses.

The Egyptian legislator will need to consider whether existing exceptions cover AI training, and if not, whether new legislation or regulatory guidance is needed.

The current legal framework was not designed for a world in which machines learn from human creativity at industrial scale. Adapting that framework is not a matter of if, but when — and how.

International Frameworks

Different jurisdictions have begun to address these issues in different ways:

European Union

The EU's Digital Single Market Directive (2019) introduced text and data mining (TDM) exceptions, but with important limitations — including allowing rightsholders to opt out of TDM for their works. The EU AI Act (2024) further adds transparency obligations for AI developers regarding training data.

United States

In the US, the doctrine of fair use has been central to the debate. Recent litigation — such as cases brought by authors and visual artists against major AI companies — is testing the boundaries of fair use in the AI context. The outcomes remain uncertain.

WIPO

The World Intellectual Property Organization has begun a multi-year dialogue on AI and IP, bringing together member states to discuss potential frameworks. As of 2026, no binding international instrument exists — but the conversation is advancing.

A global patchwork

The absence of a unified international approach creates uncertainty for creators, AI developers, and rights holders alike. A creator in Egypt, a developer in the US, and a user in Europe may all be subject to different rules governing the same AI system.

Proposed Solutions

A balanced approach is needed — one that protects creators while allowing innovation to continue. This paper proposes a framework built on the following principles:

1. Mandatory Transparency

AI developers should be required to disclose, in a publicly accessible form, the categories of works used to train their models. This does not necessarily mean revealing every individual work, but it does mean providing meaningful information about sources.

2. Meaningful Opt-Out Mechanisms

Creators should have the ability to opt out of AI training before their works are used — not after. This requires technical standards and industry cooperation to make opt-out signals effective.

3. Fair Compensation Models

Where works are used for AI training, and where such use is permitted by law or by the creator's choice, compensation models should be developed. These could take the form of licensing agreements, collective management, or statutory remuneration schemes.

4. Balanced Exceptions

Exceptions for AI training should be carefully scoped — allowing legitimate research and innovation while preventing wholesale commercial exploitation of creators' works without compensation.

5. Moral Rights Protection

Moral rights must be preserved. AI outputs should not misattribute works, distort them in ways that harm the creator's reputation, or erase the connection between a creator and their work.

The balance we need

IP law has always been about balance — between rewarding creators and enabling the spread of knowledge. The AI era requires us to find that balance again, in a world where machines learn from everything humans have made.

Conclusion

The rise of AI as a "digital educator" is not a temporary phenomenon — it is a structural shift in how knowledge and creativity are processed, reproduced, and monetized. The legal frameworks that govern intellectual property must evolve accordingly.

This paper has argued for a balanced approach — grounded in transparency, meaningful opt-out rights, fair compensation, and the preservation of moral rights. Such an approach does not pit creators against innovation; it recognizes that sustainable innovation depends on a healthy, protected creative ecosystem.

For Egypt, this means reviewing Law No. 82 of 2002 in light of AI developments, and engaging actively in international discussions at WIPO and other forums. For creators, it means that their rights are not a relic of the pre-AI era, but a foundation for the AI era to come.

The question is not whether AI will continue to learn from human creativity — it will. The question is whether the law will ensure that this learning happens fairly, transparently, and with respect for the people whose work made it possible.
