IP Due Diligence in M&A Transactions: What Buyers Miss

Walid Mohamed Abu Al-Ela November 2025 8 min read

In many M&A transactions, the target's most valuable assets are its intangible ones — brands, patents, software, customer data, trade secrets. Yet intellectual property often receives the least rigorous scrutiny during due diligence. This is a costly pattern. Here's what buyers typically miss, and how to avoid it.

Why IP Due Diligence Matters

M&A buyers scrutinize financials, liabilities, contracts, and tax positions with great care. But IP is often treated as a "support" element — reviewed superficially, or deferred to post-signing. This is a mistake, because IP issues discovered after closing are:

  • Expensive to fix — remediating ownership gaps or obtaining missing assignments can take months;
  • Impossible to unwind — you cannot "return" a trade secret that has already been disclosed or a trademark that has been opposed;
  • Difficult to value — unverified IP claims complicate post-deal accounting and synergy planning;
  • Legally disruptive — a challenge to a core patent or trademark can invalidate the rationale of the deal.

In technology, media, pharmaceutical, and consumer-facing deals — the majority of high-value transactions — IP is the asset. Treating it as a footnote is a strategy for unpleasant surprises.

Key principle

If the deal's value proposition depends on IP — brands, technology, content, know-how — then IP due diligence is not a workstream. It is the deal.

The Core IP Assets to Review

IP due diligence should systematically cover all relevant categories of IP. The following are typically the most critical:

Trademarks

Registered and unregistered marks, applications, renewals, and territorial coverage.

Patents

Granted patents, pending applications, PCT filings, and their claim scope.

Copyrights

Software code, content, creative works, and their chain of title.

Trade Secrets

Know-how, formulas, algorithms, and the controls protecting them.

Industrial Designs

Registered and unregistered designs, and territorial protection.

Domain Names

Registrations, renewals, and their alignment with the business.

Data & Databases

Customer data, proprietary datasets, and compliance with data protection.

Licenses & Agreements

In-licenses, out-licenses, assignments, and their transferability on a change of control.

Ownership: The Silent Risk

The most common — and most under-estimated — IP due diligence problem is ownership. The target may use, sell, or rely on IP that it does not actually own. Common gaps include:

3.1 Contractor-Developed Works

Where software, designs, or content were developed by external contractors, ownership may belong to the contractor unless there is a written assignment. Under Egyptian law — as in many jurisdictions — copyright and IP ownership do not automatically transfer to the commissioning party.

3.2 Employee-Developed Works

Employee-created IP may be owned by the employer — but this depends on the employment relationship, the scope of duties, and the applicable law. Inconsistencies between different contracts create ownership uncertainty.

3.3 Joint Development and Co-Ownership

Where IP was co-developed with partners or joint ventures, ownership may be shared. Co-ownership typically requires consent to license or transfer — which can complicate post-deal integration.

3.4 Chain of Title Gaps

Even where IP was properly transferred in the past, missing documentation — or unrecorded assignments — can create chain of title gaps that emerge only when enforcement is attempted.

Ownership gap = deal risk

If the seller cannot demonstrate clear, documented ownership of core IP, the buyer is acquiring a legal dispute waiting to happen — not a clean asset. This justifies either price adjustment or deal restructuring.

Liens, Licenses & Encumbrances

Even where ownership is clear, IP may be subject to encumbrances that restrict its value or usability. Key items to review:

  • Security interests — IP pledged as collateral for loans, requiring release or buyer consent;
  • Exclusive licenses — granting third parties rights that exclude or limit the buyer's use;
  • Non-exclusive licenses — allowing third parties to use IP, potentially undermining differentiation;
  • Cross-licenses — mutual licensing arrangements that survive the transaction and affect post-deal freedom to operate;
  • Covenants not to sue — waivers of enforcement that may limit protection against competitors;
  • Assignments to third parties — where partial or conditional transfers have been made, creating co-ownership or use conflicts.

Each of these may survive a change of control — and each may affect the deal's value or post-closing strategy. Reviewing them properly requires not just a legal review but also a commercial understanding of how the IP is used in the business.

Common Red Flags

Beyond the general categories, certain specific patterns should trigger closer scrutiny:

Red Flags to Watch For

  • No written assignments from founders, contractors, or key employees;
  • Trademarks registered in a personal name or related entity, not the target company;
  • Pending litigation, oppositions, or cancellation proceedings involving core IP;
  • Patents filed but not yet granted, with uncertain claim scope;
  • Unregistered but commercially important trade secrets — with weak internal controls;
  • Open-source software embedded in proprietary products, creating compliance issues;
  • Domain names registered to third parties or former employees;
  • Customer data collected without proper consents or registrations.

Any of these should trigger deeper investigation. Many can be resolved — but the cost and timeline must be understood before signing, not after.

IP due diligence is not about finding problems. It's about pricing and structuring the deal around the reality of the assets — not the seller's assumptions.

Buyer Protection Mechanisms

Where IP issues are identified, buyers have several tools to protect their position. These are typically negotiated in the SPA (sale and purchase agreement):

6.1 Representations & Warranties

Detailed IP representations from the seller — covering ownership, validity, non-infringement, and absence of claims. These form the basis for post-closing recourse if the representations prove false.

6.2 Indemnities

Specific indemnity provisions for identified IP risks — for example, indemnification for losses arising from a specific challenge to a trademark or patent.

6.3 Purchase Price Adjustments

Where material IP issues exist, the purchase price may be adjusted downward, or a portion of the price may be held in escrow pending resolution.

6.4 Earn-Outs

Tying part of the consideration to post-closing IP-related milestones — such as successful patent prosecution or trademark registration — aligns incentives and defers payment until value is delivered.

6.5 Conditions Precedent

Where IP issues are significant, closing may be conditioned on remediation — such as obtaining assignments, releasing encumbrances, or resolving pending oppositions.

The structuring principle

IP due diligence should inform not just price but structure. In many deals, the right answer is not a lower price — it's a smarter deal: escrows, earn-outs, or conditional closings that align with the actual IP risk.

Conclusion

IP is often the most valuable asset in an M&A transaction — and often the least rigorously examined. The cost of that gap can be significant: price erosion, post-closing disputes, unexpected integration costs, and — in the worst case — a deal that fails to deliver what it promised.

The practical checklist:

  • Inventory all IP — trademarks, patents, copyrights, trade secrets, designs, domain names, data, and agreements;
  • Verify ownership — from founders, employees, and contractors, with documentation;
  • Identify encumbrances — liens, licenses, and covenants that may survive closing;
  • Screen for red flags — pending disputes, unregistered assets, open-source risks, data issues;
  • Structure protection — representations, indemnities, price adjustments, escrows, and conditions precedent;
  • Value accordingly — make sure the price and structure reflect the actual state of the IP assets.

In short: IP due diligence is not a compliance exercise. It is a valuation and risk exercise. Done well, it protects the buyer, informs the price, and positions the deal for success.
