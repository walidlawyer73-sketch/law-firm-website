Preparing for Commercial Litigation: A Practical Checklist for Business Owners

Walid Mohamed Abu Al-Ela December 2025 7 min read

Commercial litigation is not just a legal event — it is a business event. It affects cash flow, relationships, reputation, and management time. And the outcome depends as much on what happens before the case is filed as on what happens in the courtroom. This guide outlines the practical preparation that gives a business the strongest possible position — whether you are filing or defending.

The First Decision: Litigate?

Before preparing for litigation, ask the most important question: is litigation the right choice? Courts are only one of several mechanisms for resolving disputes. Others include:

  • Negotiation — direct talks between the parties, often leading to a commercial settlement;
  • Mediation — a structured process with a neutral mediator, often faster and cheaper than litigation;
  • Arbitration — binding adjudication by private arbitrators, if your contract provides for it;
  • Bankruptcy proceedings — in some debt-related cases, a strategic alternative;
  • Enforcement action — if you already hold a judgment, arbitration award, or a negotiable instrument.

Factors that typically favor litigation: (a) a clear legal right that is being violated, (b) an opponent who refuses good-faith negotiation, (c) a matter of principle or precedent, (d) urgent need for interim relief. Factors that favor alternatives: (a) a long-term commercial relationship worth preserving, (b) uncertainty about the legal position, (c) high cost relative to the amount in dispute, (d) confidentiality concerns.

Consider this first

Litigation is a tool, not a goal. The best commercial lawyers always ask: what business outcome do we actually want? — and then choose the path that gets there efficiently.

Documents to Gather

Whether you are filing or defending, documents are the backbone of a commercial case. A well-organized file saves months of procedural manoeuvring. Key documents typically include:

Contractual Documents

  • The signed contract (and any annexes, schedules, or addenda);
  • Any subsequent amendments or side letters;
  • Purchase orders, invoices, and delivery notes;
  • Payment receipts, bank transfers, and financial records;
  • Any guarantees, security, or collateral arrangements.

Correspondence & Communications

  • Emails between the parties relating to the dispute;
  • WhatsApp or other messaging records (with proper authentication);
  • Formal notices, demand letters, and replies;
  • Meeting minutes or notes where relevant;
  • Any recorded calls or transcripts (subject to admissibility rules).

Corporate & Commercial Records

  • Commercial registration, tax card, and articles of association;
  • Board resolutions or shareholder approvals relevant to the transaction;
  • Powers of attorney for signatories;
  • Financial statements that demonstrate the loss or claim;
  • Audit reports or expert valuations where applicable.

Evidence of Damage / Loss

  • Invoices and receipts showing additional costs incurred;
  • Lost-profit calculations with supporting documentation;
  • Evidence of reputational or commercial harm (where provable);
  • Expert reports on technical or financial matters.

Evidence Preparation

Gathering documents is one thing — preparing admissible evidence is another. Egyptian courts have specific requirements on how evidence is presented. Key principles:

3.1 Authenticity

Documents must be authentic. For electronic documents (emails, WhatsApp messages), be prepared to demonstrate origin and integrity — often through technical certification or a formal forensic report.

3.2 Timeliness

Evidence should be organized from the outset. In many cases, you cannot introduce new documents late in the process unless there is a valid justification.

3.3 Relevance and Overlap

More documents are not always better. A focused set of high-quality evidence is usually more persuasive than a pile of unorganized material. Prepare a clear narrative that connects each document to the legal claims.

3.4 Expert Evidence

Technical, financial, or industry-specific matters often require expert reports. Under Egyptian procedure, court-appointed experts are common — but party-appointed experts can also be used. Selecting the right expert and defining their scope early is critical.

Preserve — don't destroy

Once a dispute is anticipated, do not destroy any potentially relevant documents or communications. Destroying evidence can seriously damage your position — and in some cases, lead to adverse inferences or even criminal liability.

What to Expect: The Stages

Commercial litigation in Egypt typically progresses through the following stages. The specific timeline varies by court (first instance, economic court, appeal) and by complexity:

1

Pre-Filing Assessment

Reviewing the claim, assessing prospects, gathering evidence, deciding whether to litigate or settle, and choosing the correct court and jurisdiction.

2

Filing the Claim

Drafting and filing the statement of claim, paying court fees, and registering the case with the appropriate court.

3

Service & Response

Formal service of process on the defendant, followed by the defendant's written response and any counterclaim.

4

Procedural Hearings

Multiple short hearings to address procedural matters, exchange documents, and set timelines.

5

Expert Phase (if applicable)

Appointment of a court expert or expert panel to review technical, financial, or industry questions and prepare a report.

6

Substantive Hearings

Presentation of pleadings, evidence, and arguments before the court.

7

Judgment

The court issues its decision — which may be subject to appeal or further proceedings.

8

Enforcement

If the judgment is not voluntarily satisfied, enforcement measures — such as attachment or garnishment — may be pursued.

Commercial litigation is rarely quick. In Egypt, first-instance cases can take a year or more, and appeals can extend timelines further. Planning for this reality — operationally and financially — is essential.

Understanding Costs

Business owners often underestimate the total cost of litigation. Beyond legal fees, expect:

  • Court fees — based on the value of the claim, payable at filing;
  • Expert fees — if a court-appointed expert is involved;
  • Documentation and translation — for certified copies and translations where needed;
  • Enforcement costs — fees for bailiffs and enforcement procedures;
  • Opportunity cost — management time and internal resources diverted;
  • Adverse cost orders — in some cases, the losing party may be ordered to pay part of the winning party's costs.

A realistic cost-benefit analysis at the outset prevents unpleasant surprises. A clear budget — broken down by stage — is a valuable management tool.

Protecting Your Business

While a case is pending, businesses should take active steps to protect their position:

✓ Preserve Documents

Implement a litigation hold: instruct all relevant personnel not to destroy any documents, emails, or records. Document the hold in writing.

✓ Protect Confidential Information

Review confidentiality arrangements with employees, contractors, and partners. Where sensitive information is disclosed in litigation, seek protective orders or in-camera proceedings where possible.

✓ Manage Communications

All communications relating to the dispute should be routed through legal counsel where appropriate. Off-the-cuff comments, internal emails, and social media posts can all become evidence.

✓ Consider Interim Measures

If the opponent may dissipate assets, relocate, or otherwise frustrate the eventual judgment, consider applying for interim measures — such as precautionary attachment or travel bans — early.

✓ Plan for Settlement

Even when litigating, keep the door open to settlement. Many commercial cases resolve mid-stream, and being prepared for a negotiated exit — at any stage — is often the most valuable strategic asset.

Conclusion

Commercial litigation is a serious business decision. Preparation, however, can make the difference between a manageable dispute and an overwhelming one.

The key takeaways:

  • Decide strategically — litigation is a tool, not a goal; consider alternatives first;
  • Gather documents early — contracts, correspondence, financial records, and evidence of loss;
  • Prepare admissible evidence — focus on authenticity, timeliness, and relevance;
  • Understand the stages — from pre-filing to enforcement, each stage has its own requirements;
  • Plan costs realistically — direct and indirect costs add up quickly;
  • Protect your position — preserve documents, manage communications, and keep settlement options open.

With the right preparation, commercial litigation becomes a controlled process — rather than a crisis. And that is what gives businesses the best chance of the outcome they want.
