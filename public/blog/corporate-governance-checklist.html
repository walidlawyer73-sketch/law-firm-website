Corporate Governance Checklist for Egyptian Companies

Walid Mohamed Abu Al-Ela January 2026 6 min read

Corporate governance is not a box to tick. It is the operating system of a well-run company — the rules that determine how decisions are made, how conflicts are resolved, and how accountability is maintained. For Egyptian companies, this means complying with the Companies Law, the Capital Market Authority regulations, and — for some entities — stock exchange requirements. This checklist breaks down what matters most.

Why Governance Matters in Egypt

For years, corporate governance in Egypt was often treated as a formality — a set of documents filed at incorporation and then forgotten. That era is ending. There are three key reasons:

  • Tighter regulatory scrutiny — the Financial Regulatory Authority (FRA) and the General Authority for Investment and Free Zones (GAFI) have intensified enforcement;
  • Investor expectations — banks, institutional investors, and foreign partners increasingly demand documented governance frameworks;
  • Legal consequences — governance failures can lead to personal liability for directors, tax penalties, and contract invalidity.

For companies planning to raise capital, secure financing, or expand into new markets, strong corporate governance is not optional — it is a competitive requirement.

The Legal Framework

Corporate governance in Egypt is governed primarily by:

  • The Companies Law (Law No. 159 of 1981) — the core legislation for joint-stock companies, LLCs, and partnerships limited by shares;
  • The Capital Market Law (Law No. 95 of 1992) — for listed companies and those operating in financial markets;
  • The Investment Law (Law No. 72 of 2017) — for investment-related entities and incentives;
  • FRA Decisions — the Financial Regulatory Authority's executive regulations and corporate governance guidelines;
  • Sector-Specific Rules — banks, insurance companies, and other regulated sectors have additional requirements.

Which law applies to you?

The answer depends on your legal form. Joint-stock companies (Shariket Mossahama) have the most extensive governance requirements. LLCs have more flexibility, but still must comply with core rules on assemblies, records, and reporting.

General Assembly Checklist

The General Assembly is the highest authority in the company. Its decisions shape corporate strategy, approve major transactions, and elect directors.

Annual General Assembly (AGM)

  • Hold the AGM within 3 months of the fiscal year end;
  • Approve the board's annual report and financial statements;
  • Approve the auditor's report;
  • Decide on distribution of profits and dividends;
  • Elect or re-elect directors as required;
  • Appoint the auditors for the coming year;
  • Discharge the board of directors from liability for the past year;
  • Document the meeting in minutes signed by attendees.

Extraordinary General Assembly (EGM)

  • Convene an EGM when required — e.g., capital increases, amendments to articles of association, mergers, or dissolution;
  • Send proper notice at least 21 days in advance (unless otherwise provided);
  • Include detailed agenda in the notice;
  • Ensure quorum requirements are met (usually 50% for first call; lower for second call);
  • Record decisions in notarized minutes;
  • File any resulting amendments with the competent authority (usually GAFI or FRA).

Documentation & Records

  • Maintain a register of shareholders (updated regularly);
  • Keep signed minutes of all general assembly meetings;
  • Retain all notices, attendance sheets, and resolutions;
  • Store records in accordance with applicable retention periods.

Board of Directors Checklist

The Board of Directors manages the company and is accountable to the General Assembly. Strong board practices are essential for both legal compliance and operational effectiveness.

Composition & Appointments

  • Ensure the board is properly elected by the General Assembly;
  • Document each director's appointment and term;
  • For listed companies, ensure compliance with independence requirements;
  • Maintain a register of directors and their interests.

Board Meetings & Decisions

  • Hold regular board meetings — at least quarterly (or as required by the articles);
  • Send proper notice with detailed agenda in advance;
  • Ensure quorum is met and documented;
  • Record decisions in minutes signed by attending directors;
  • Address conflicts of interest — directors with a conflict must disclose and recuse themselves where required;
  • Maintain a decisions log for audit purposes.

Board Committees (For Larger Entities)

  • Establish an Audit Committee — for listed companies and many regulated entities;
  • Consider a Risk Committee — for entities with significant financial exposure;
  • Consider a Nomination & Remuneration Committee — for listed companies;
  • Define each committee's charter, authority, and reporting obligations;
  • Document all committee meetings and recommendations.

Good governance is not about paperwork. It is about building a company that can withstand scrutiny, attract investment, and make decisions with clarity and accountability.

Compliance & Filing Checklist

Beyond internal governance, companies must comply with ongoing regulatory obligations. These vary by legal form and sector, but the following are common to most Egyptian companies:

Annual Filings

  • File annual financial statements with the competent authority;
  • File corporate tax returns with the Egyptian Tax Authority;
  • File VAT returns (monthly or quarterly, depending on registration);
  • File payroll tax and social insurance returns;
  • Renew commercial registration and tax card as required.

Structural Compliance

  • Keep articles of association up to date with actual operations;
  • File amendments (capital, activities, addresses) with GAFI/FRA promptly;
  • Maintain valid licenses for regulated activities;
  • Ensure any foreign ownership complies with sector-specific limits.

Employment Compliance

  • Maintain written employment contracts for all employees;
  • Ensure registration with the National Organization for Social Insurance;
  • Comply with minimum wage and labor law requirements;
  • Keep up-to-date personnel files and workplace policies.

Governance Documentation

Sound corporate governance depends on clear, well-maintained documentation. Whether you are a small LLC or a large joint-stock company, the following documents form the backbone of your governance framework:

  • Articles of Association — your foundational constitutional document;
  • Internal Bylaws / Board Rules — how the board operates, meeting procedures, and decision rules;
  • Delegation of Authority Matrix — who can sign for what, and up to which amount;
  • Board and Committee Charters — for established committees;
  • Conflict of Interest Policy — for directors, officers, and key employees;
  • Code of Conduct — ethical standards for the company;
  • Confidentiality & Data Protection Policies — for handling sensitive information;
  • Register of Shareholders & Directors — updated continuously.

Documentation tip

Do not treat governance documents as static. Review them at least once a year — and after any material change (capital increase, restructuring, sector regulation). Outdated documents can create as many problems as missing ones.

Conclusion

Corporate governance in Egypt is no longer a formality. It is a legal requirement, an investor expectation, and — above all — a way to build a company that can grow with confidence.

The key takeaways from this checklist:

  • Comply with the framework — Companies Law, Capital Market Law, FRA regulations, and sector-specific rules;
  • Run clean assemblies — proper notice, quorum, minutes, and filings;
  • Operate a functioning board — regular meetings, documented decisions, conflict-of-interest management;
  • Keep filings current — annual reports, tax returns, and structural updates;
  • Document everything — articles, bylaws, policies, and registers.

For founders, directors, and executives, the effort invested in governance today pays off tomorrow — in smoother financing, stronger partnerships, and greater resilience.
